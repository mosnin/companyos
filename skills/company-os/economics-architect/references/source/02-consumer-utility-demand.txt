ECONOMICS ARCHITECT DOCTRINE — SECTION 02: Consumer Theory: Utility, Demand, Budget Lines, and Willingness to Pay

Purpose
Translate microeconomics and macroeconomics into operating decisions for building and scaling businesses.
This doctrine is action-oriented: pricing, incentives, product strategy, market selection, capacity planning,
competitive dynamics, and macro-aware forecasting.

Core Economic Lens for Business
1) Scarcity and tradeoffs: every choice has an opportunity cost.
2) Incentives and behavior: systems produce outcomes.
3) Margins: decisions happen at the margin (marginal cost vs marginal benefit).
4) Markets: prices coordinate supply and demand under constraints.
5) Information: asymmetric information creates failures and opportunities.
6) Externalities: costs/benefits often spill over beyond the transaction.
7) Cycles: macro conditions shift demand, costs, and risk.

Operating Rules
1) Always define the unit of analysis (customer, order, cohort, market segment, time period).
2) Convert narratives into numbers: demand curves, elasticities, unit economics, and constraints.
3) Separate price from value: price is a lever; value is the driver.
4) Use experiments to estimate uncertain parameters (elasticity, willingness to pay, churn response).
5) Keep models simple enough to guide decisions, strict enough to falsify.

============================================================
Scope
How customers choose: utility, demand, budget constraints, and value measurement.

1) Utility Theory (Business Translation)
Utility is the satisfaction or value a customer derives from consumption.
Decompose utility into:
• functional utility (job done)
• emotional utility (confidence, relief)
• social utility (status, belonging)
• option value (flexibility)
• risk utility (certainty vs uncertainty)

2) Law of Demand
All else equal, quantity demanded decreases as price increases.
Business implication:
• every price change shifts conversion and the buyer mix
• measure response by segment and channel, not just in aggregate

3) Budget Lines and Constraints
Customers face constraints:
• money
• time
• attention
• organizational approvals
Business implication:
• “affordable” includes time-to-value and implementation effort
• reduce non-monetary costs to increase demand without lowering price

4) Marginal Utility and Diminishing Returns
Marginal utility decreases as consumption increases.
Business applications:
• tiering and packaging: segments value “more” differently
• usage-based pricing aligns price with marginal utility
• bundles can increase total utility by reducing decision cost

5) Estimating Willingness to Pay (WTP)
Methods:
• direct: interviews with tradeoff questions (careful about bias)
• revealed preference: observe choices at different prices
• A/B price tests (guardrails for brand)
• conjoint analysis (for attribute tradeoffs)
Outputs:
• WTP distribution
• segment-specific WTP
• value drivers that shift WTP

Deliverables
1) utility decomposition for your ICP
2) constraint reduction plan (time, risk, effort)
3) WTP estimation plan and measurement guardrails
4) packaging hypotheses tied to marginal utility
