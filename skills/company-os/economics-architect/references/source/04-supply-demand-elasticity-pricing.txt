ECONOMICS ARCHITECT DOCTRINE — SECTION 04: Supply and Demand, Elasticity, and Pricing Architecture

Purpose
Translate microeconomics and macroeconomics into operating decisions for building and scaling businesses.
This doctrine is action-oriented: pricing, incentives, product strategy, market selection, capacity planning,
competitive dynamics, and macro-aware forecasting.

Core Economic Lens for Business
1) Scarcity and tradeoffs: every choice has an opportunity cost.
2) Incentives and behavior: systems produce outcomes.
3) Margins: decisions happen at the margin (marginal cost vs marginal benefit).
4) Markets: prices coordinate supply and demand under constraints.
5) Information: asymmetric information creates failures and opportunities.
6) Externalities: costs/benefits often spill over beyond the transaction.
7) Cycles: macro conditions shift demand, costs, and risk.

Operating Rules
1) Always define the unit of analysis (customer, order, cohort, market segment, time period).
2) Convert narratives into numbers: demand curves, elasticities, unit economics, and constraints.
3) Separate price from value: price is a lever; value is the driver.
4) Use experiments to estimate uncertain parameters (elasticity, willingness to pay, churn response).
5) Keep models simple enough to guide decisions, strict enough to falsify.

============================================================
Scope
Market pricing mechanics: demand curves, elasticity, surplus, and pricing strategy.

1) Supply and Demand as Business Tools
Demand: how many units customers buy at each price.
Supply: how many units the firm can provide at each cost/capacity.
Equilibrium: where quantity supplied meets quantity demanded.
Business use:
• predict effects of price changes
• forecast volumes under constraints
• understand how capacity limits can raise effective prices (scarcity)

2) Elasticity
Price elasticity of demand (PED): responsiveness of quantity to price.
• elastic: demand changes a lot with price
• inelastic: demand changes little with price
Business implications:
• elastic categories require differentiation and segmentation
• inelastic categories can support price increases if value and trust are strong
Perfectly inelastic or elastic are theoretical extremes; real markets are in between.

3) Substitutes and Cross-Elasticity
Substitute goods constrain pricing.
Cross-price elasticity tells how demand shifts when competitors change price.
Business use:
• map your true substitutes (including “do nothing”)
• differentiate to reduce substitutability

4) Consumer Surplus and Producer Surplus
Consumer surplus: value above price.
Producer surplus: price above cost.
Business use:
• capture surplus via better targeting, packaging, and pricing discrimination (ethical and legal constraints)
• preserve consumer surplus where retention depends on perceived fairness

5) Deadweight Loss
When prices or quantities are distorted, total welfare falls.
Business translation:
• friction in onboarding, procurement, and UX creates deadweight loss in your funnel
Reduce friction to recover lost transactions without lowering price.

6) Pricing Architecture (Implementation)
Key choices:
• pricing metric (per seat, per usage, per outcome, per project)
• packaging (tiers, bundles, add-ons)
• discount policy (give-get trades)
• fences (limits that separate segments)
• price increases and grandfathering strategy
Measurement:
• conversion rate by price point
• churn/retention by cohort
• gross margin and payback

Deliverables
1) demand curve hypothesis and test plan
2) elasticity measurement plan (segment by segment)
3) substitutes map (including do nothing)
4) pricing architecture document (metric, tiers, fences, discount rules)
