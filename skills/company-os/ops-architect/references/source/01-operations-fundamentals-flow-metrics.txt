OPERATIONS ARCHITECT DOCTRINE — SECTION 01: Operations Fundamentals, Process Flow, and Flow Metrics

Purpose
Create an expert-level operations playbook for designing, measuring, and improving systems that move work through processes.
This doctrine is built for execution: convert messy reality into flow models, constraints, queue designs, inventory policies,
forecasting systems, supply chain structures, and delivery cadences.

Core Model
1) Work flows through a process; the process has capacity and variability.
2) Variability creates queues; queues create time.
3) Inventory is a buffer against variability, but has costs.
4) Constraints determine throughput; improvement must focus on the constraint.
5) Delivery is governed by network logic (critical path), resource limits, and feedback loops.

Operating Rules
1) Optimize the system, not local steps.
2) Measure flow and variability before changing structure.
3) Improve the bottleneck first; subordinate everything else.
4) Use policies (batching, reorder points, WIP limits) to control variability.
5) Prefer simple, stable policies that survive estimation error.

============================================================
Scope
Operations management is the design and control of systems that transform inputs into outputs.
This section formalizes process flows and the metrics that govern performance.

1) Process Flow Diagram (PFD)
Core elements
1) Flow units: orders, patients, tickets, parts, leads, packages
2) Activities: work steps, approvals, inspections, transformations
3) Buffers: waiting areas between activities (queues, inventories, backlogs)
4) Routing: paths units can take (deterministic or probabilistic)
5) Rework loops: quality failures returning work upstream

Build a PFD
1) Define the flow unit precisely
2) List activities in actual execution order, including handoffs
3) Insert buffers explicitly at every wait or batching point
4) Mark routing probabilities and rework rates
5) Annotate each activity with processing time distribution and capacity

2) The Big 3 Flow Metrics
1) Inventory (I): number of flow units within the system (WIP or backlog)
2) Flow time (T): average time a unit spends end-to-end
3) Flow rate (R): average output rate (throughput)

Little’s Law
I = R × T
Use as consistency check and design lever.

3) Capacity and Utilization
1) Capacity: maximum sustainable output per time, given resources and policies
2) Utilization (u): load divided by capacity

Key insight
As u approaches 1, waiting time typically increases nonlinearly under variability.

4) Demand Constrained vs Supply Constrained
1) Demand constrained: demand below capacity; optimize responsiveness and cost
2) Supply constrained: demand above capacity; optimize constraint and prioritization

Diagnosis checklist
1) Persistent backlog and long waits suggests supply constrained
2) Frequent idleness suggests demand constrained
3) Volatile lead times suggests variability and queue design problems

5) Data to collect first
1) Arrival rate by time bucket
2) Processing time distributions per activity (not only averages)
3) Rework rate and defect sources
4) Setup times and batch constraints
5) WIP levels and queue lengths per buffer

Deliverables
1) Process flow diagram with activities and buffers
2) Baseline table for I, T, R, capacity, utilization at each step
3) Constraint hypothesis and measurement plan
